=== 1.28.23
The edges are a–b, a–d, b–c, b–e, d–e, d–g, c–e, c–f, e–f, e–g and f–g.

1. **a b e f d g c** ✘. After f, DFS must go to an unvisited neighbour of f (c or g). d is not adjacent to f.
2. **a b e f c g d** ✔. a→b→e→f→c. c has no new neighbours, so back up to f→g→d.
3. **a d g e b c f** ✔. a→d→g→e→b→c→f, and every step is an edge.
4. **a d b c g e f** ✘. b is not adjacent to d, and d still has unvisited neighbours (e, g).

**2 and 3 only**, **option B**.

=== 1.30.2
**(i)** Each new element must be checked against its chain, to avoid duplicates or to keep the chain sorted. The $i$-th insertion may scan a chain of length $i-1$. The worst case is
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i-1) = O(n^2)$$
(If you always insert at the head without checking, each insert is $O(1)$ and the total is $O(n)$.)

**(ii)** The worst case happens when **every key hashes to the same bucket**, so all $n$ elements form one chain, effectively a linked list.

=== 1.30.5
Under simple uniform hashing, each key lands in any given slot with probability $1/m$. That holds whether $h_1$ (odd keys) or $h_2$ (even keys) is used. By linearity, the expected number of keys in a slot is $n\cdot\frac1m = \frac nm$. **Option B**.
=== 1.30.6
Any **fixed** hash function, whether division or multiplication, can be defeated. An adversary who knows it can pick $k$ keys that all collide.

**Universal hashing** chooses the hash function at random from a universal family at run time. Whatever keys the adversary picks, the expected number of collisions stays small. **Option C**.
