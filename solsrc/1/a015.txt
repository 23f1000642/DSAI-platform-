=== 1.32.15
This is the Babylonian (Newton) method. It keeps $x\cdot y = m$ with one value above $\sqrt m$ and one below, and replaces $x$ by the average of the two. Both converge to $\sqrt m$, and the loop stops once they're within $\epsilon$.

It prints approximately $m^{1/2}$, **option C**.

=== 1.32.18

=== 1.32.20
$f$ adds an even element and subtracts the rest of the computation after an odd one. Work from the inside out:

| call | element | value |
|---|---|---|
| f(…, 1) | 6 (even) | 6 + 0 = 6 |
| f(…, 2) | 11 (odd) | 11 − 6 = 5 |
| f(…, 3) | 4 (even) | 4 + 5 = 9 |
| f(…, 4) | 13 (odd) | 13 − 9 = 4 |
| f(…, 5) | 7 (odd) | 7 − 4 = 3 |
| f(a, 6) | 12 (even) | 12 + 3 = **15** |

**Option C**.

=== 1.32.23
- The outer loop runs $i = n/2 \dots n$, which is about $n/2$ iterations.
- The inner loop doubles $j$ from 2 to $n$, which is about $\log n$ iterations.
- Each inner iteration adds $n/2$.

$k \approx \frac n2\cdot\log n\cdot\frac n2 = \Theta(n^2\log n)$. **Option B**.
=== 1.32.24
$Y$ starts as the sum of the whole array. The nested loops then compute the sum $Z$ of **every** subarray $E[i..j]$ and keep the maximum.

So MyX returns the **maximum subarray sum**, **option A**. It's a brute-force $O(n^3)$ version of Kadane's algorithm.
