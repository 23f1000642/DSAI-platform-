=== 1.27.11
The first loop eliminates candidates. Keep a candidate $i$, and scan $j > i$:

- If $A[i][j] = 0$, then $j$ can't be a sink, since $i$ doesn't point to it. Skip $j$: **E1 = `!A[i][j]`**.
- If $A[i][j] = 1$, then $i$ has an outgoing edge, so $i$ can't be a sink. Make $j$ the new candidate: **E2 = `i = j`**.

Every vertex other than the final $i$ gets eliminated, so only $i$ can possibly be a sink. **Option C**.
=== 1.27.12
The second loop checks that the candidate $i$ really is a sink. For every $j\ne i$, two things must hold: no edge $i\to j$, so `A[i][j] == 0`, and an edge $j\to i$, so `A[j][i] == 1`.

The flag must be cleared if **either** condition fails:
$$E_3 = A[i][j] \;\|\; !A[j][i]$$
**Option D**.

=== 1.28.1
**Tree edges (T):** 1→2, 2→5, 5→3, 5→4, 5→6, 6→7, 7→8.

So the DFS tree is 1 – 2 – 5, and 5 has children 3, 4 and 6. Below 6 hangs the chain 6 – 7 – 8.

**Non-tree edges.** Classify each one by the tree relationship between its endpoints:

| edge | relation in the DFS tree | type |
|---|---|---|
| 1→3 | 3 is a descendant of 1 | **forward** |
| 2→4 | 4 is a descendant of 2 | **forward** |
| 4→5 | 5 is an ancestor of 4 | **back** |
| 4→6 | 6 is in a different (earlier) subtree | **cross** |
| 4→8 | 8 is in 6's subtree | **cross** |
| 3→7 | 7 is in 6's subtree | **cross** |
| 3→8 | 8 is in 6's subtree | **cross** |

**Forward edges:** 1→3, 2→4. **Back edge:** 4→5. **Cross edges:** 4→6, 4→8, 3→7, 3→8.

(Cross edges always point from a later-discovered subtree to an earlier one, so 6's subtree was explored before 3 and 4.)

=== 1.28.3
Suppose $v$ is the next new vertex after $u$.

- If $u$ has an unvisited neighbour, DFS goes to it directly. Then $\{u,v\}$ is an edge and $v$ is $u$'s child.
- If $\{u,v\}$ is **not** an edge, $u$ had no unvisited neighbours when it was visited. So $u$ gets no children: **$u$ is a leaf** of $T$. DFS backtracks to some ancestor that discovers $v$.

C is always true. A and B fail when $u$ is a leaf. D fails because $v$ may hang off any ancestor of $u$, not necessarily $u$'s parent. **Option C**.
=== 1.28.4
BFS visits vertices in non-decreasing order of distance from $r$: the whole of layer $k$ comes before layer $k+1$. So if $u$ is visited before $v$, then $d(r,u) \le d(r,v)$. They can be equal when both are in the same layer, so strict "<" isn't guaranteed. **Option C**.
=== 1.28.5
The edges are a–b, a–e, a–f, b–e, b–f, b–h, e–g, f–h, f–g, g–h.

- I. **a b e g h f**: a→b→e→g→h→f. Each step follows an edge from the deepest vertex that still has an unvisited neighbour. ✔
- II. **a b f e h g**: after a→b→f, the next vertex should come from f's neighbours (h or g). e isn't adjacent to f. Backtracking to b is allowed only when f has no unvisited neighbours, but h and g are unvisited. ✘
- III. **a b f h g e**: a→b→f→h→g→e (g–e is an edge). ✔
- IV. **a f g h b e**: a→f→g→h→b (h–b is an edge) →e (b–e). ✔

I, III and IV, **option D**.
=== 1.28.6
In a DFS tree of an undirected graph, every non-tree edge is a back edge. It joins a vertex to one of its ancestors.

$u$ is a leaf, so all its non-tree neighbours are **ancestors** of $u$. Since $\deg u \ge 2$, it has at least one back edge besides the edge to its parent. Let $w$ be the ancestor of $u$ that is highest in the tree (closest to the root). The back edge $u$–$w$ plus the tree path $w \to \dots \to u$ forms a cycle. That path passes through **every** ancestor between $w$ and $u$, which includes all of $u$'s neighbours.

So there's a cycle containing $u$ and all its neighbours: **D** must hold.

A, B and C can fail: the two leaves may lie in unrelated parts of the graph.
