=== 1.42.16
The edges are $(v_i, v_{i+1})$ with weight $2i+1$, and $(v_i, v_{i+2})$ with weight $2i+2$.

Kruskal takes them in order 3, 4, 5, 6, …:

- $(v_1,v_2)$ = 3 ✔
- $(v_1,v_3)$ = 4 ✔
- $(v_2,v_3)$ = 5 ✘, a cycle
- $(v_2,v_4)$ = 6 ✔
- $(v_3,v_4)$ = 7 ✘, a cycle
- $(v_3,v_5)$ = 8 ✔
- …

So each $v_k$ for $k \ge 3$ joins through $(v_{k-2}, v_k)$, with weight $2k-2$:
$$3 + \sum_{k=3}^{n}(2k-2) = 3 + 2\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{2} - 1\right) = n^2 - n + 1$$
Check with $n = 4$: $3 + 4 + 6 = 13 = 16 - 4 + 1$ ✔. **Option B**.

=== 1.42.18
All weights are greater than 1, so squaring keeps their **order**. Kruskal makes the same choices, so $T' = T$.

=== 1.42.23
An MST of $K_4$ has 3 edges. The two lightest edges, 1 and 2, can never form a cycle, so they're always in the MST.

To push the third MST edge as high as possible, make edges 1, 2 and **3** form a triangle. Then 3 is rejected. The fourth vertex must be reached by an edge of weight at least 4: put 4 on it, and 5 and 6 elsewhere.

=== 1.42.24
- **II is true.** This is the cycle property: with distinct weights, the heaviest edge of any cycle is in no MST.
- **I is false.** Take a triangle x–y (5), y–z (1), z–x (2), plus a vertex w with x–w (7) and w–y (8). Edge x–y is the **lightest** edge on the cycle x–y–w–x (5 < 7, 8). But it's the **heaviest** on the triangle x–y–z, so the cycle property excludes it from the MST.
