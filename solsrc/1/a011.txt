=== 1.28.8
With an adjacency matrix, finding the neighbours of a vertex means scanning its whole row of $n$ entries. Each of the $n$ vertices is processed once, so the total is $n\cdot n = \Theta(n^2)$, whatever $m$ is. **Option C**.
=== 1.28.9
In an unweighted graph, BFS from $W$ reaches each vertex first along a shortest path. So the BFS tree's path from $W$ to any vertex $x$ is a shortest $W$–$x$ path, for **every** vertex, not just the leaves. It says nothing about paths between other pairs. **Option B**.
=== 1.28.10
Vertices: a left $3\times3$ grid (9), a right $3\times3$ grid (9), a bottom connector vertex between them, a pendant vertex above the connector, and a pendant to the right of the right grid's middle row. That's 21 in total.

Maximum recursion depth is the length of the longest simple path DFS can follow.

- A $3\times3$ grid is bipartite, with 5 "corner/centre" vertices and 4 "edge-middle" vertices. A Hamiltonian path through all 9 must **start and end at corner/centre** vertices.
- Cover all 9 of the left grid, ending at its bottom-right corner, which joins the connector: 9 + 1.
- Enter the right grid at its bottom-left corner. The right pendant hangs off an **edge-middle** vertex, so you can't cover all 9 and then exit to it. Either cover 9 and stop, or cover 8 and add the pendant. Both give 9.
- The top pendant can't also be included, because it's a dead end off the connector.

The maximum depth is $9 + 1 + 9 = \mathbf{19}$.
=== 1.28.11
In BFS, for any edge $(u,v)$, $|d(u) - d(v)| \le 1$. If $v$ were 2 or more levels deeper than $u$, it would have been discovered from $u$ one level sooner.

So a non-tree edge can join vertices in the same level (difference 0) or adjacent levels ($\pm1$), but never vertices **2** levels apart. **Option D**.
=== 1.28.12
BFS visits level by level. Vertex $t$ at depth 4 gets the largest possible index when every earlier level is full, and $t$ is the **last** vertex of a full level 4.

- Levels 0–3 of a full binary tree: $1+2+4+8 = 15$ vertices
- Level 4: 16 vertices

The maximum is $n = 15 + 16 = \mathbf{31}$.

=== 1.28.14
- **I is true.** In a DFS of an *undirected* graph, every non-tree edge joins a vertex to an ancestor or descendant (it's a back edge). There are no cross edges.
- **II is false.** A BFS non-tree edge may join two vertices on the **same** level, for example the third edge of a triangle, so $|i-j| = 0$ is possible.

=== 1.28.15
There are $2^3 = 8$ vertices. $\emptyset$ is a proper subset of every non-empty set, so it's adjacent to all **7** other vertices.

BFS from $\emptyset$ puts all 7 into the first level, and they can be visited in any order. After that nothing is left, so the order within the level is the only choice.

$|\mathcal B(\emptyset)| = 7! = \mathbf{5040}$.
=== 1.28.16
$T$ is both a DFS tree and a BFS tree. Since it's a BFS tree, any edge $u\to w$ has $\text{depth}(w) \le \text{depth}(u)+1$.

- **Forward edge:** this would go to a descendant at depth at least $\text{depth}(u)+2$, which is impossible. So there are **no forward edges**. ✔
- **Back edges** are possible: an edge from a node to the root keeps both tree properties.
- **Cross edges** are possible: take $v\to a$, $v\to b$, $b\to a$. DFS visits $a$ then $b$, and $b\to a$ is a cross edge. $T$ is also the BFS tree.
- D is therefore false.

Answer: **C only**.
=== 1.28.17

=== 1.28.19
The official answer is **A (tree edge)**. The intended reasoning: $v$ is farther from $s$ than $u$. When the edge is first explored from $u$, $v$ is expected to be still unvisited, so DFS goes to $v$ and the edge becomes a tree edge.

In an undirected DFS, an edge explored first from $u$ is either a tree edge (v unvisited) or a back edge ($v$ a gray ancestor that hasn't yet scanned this edge). It can't be a cross edge, and "gray" is a vertex colour, not an edge type. Strictly speaking, a back edge can occur too. For example, $u$ adjacent to $s$ and $v$ reached by a longer route. GATE's key took the tree-edge case.

=== 1.28.22
In a DAG there are no back edges. For any edge $(u,v)$:

- $v$ unvisited (tree edge): $v$ finishes before $u$.
- $v$ already finished (forward or cross edge): it finished earlier still.

Either way, **$f[u] > f[v]$**. This is why decreasing finish time gives a topological order. The discovery-time options can fail, for example with a cross edge where $d[v] < d[u]$. **Option D**.
