=== 1.55.3
For a fixed $y \ne 1$, `Count(1024, y)` halves $x$ from 1024 down to 1. That prints **10** stars, since $1024 = 2^{10}$. Then it decrements $y$ and starts over with $x = 1024$.

This repeats for $y = 1024, 1023, \dots, 2$, which is 1023 values. It stops when $y = 1$.

The total is $1023\times10 = \mathbf{10230}$.
=== 1.55.4
Each call subtracts $q = 10$ from $x$, from $y$, or (in the general case) branches into both. A leaf is reached when both are at most 0, and it returns 10.

Going from 15 to at most 0 takes exactly 2 subtractions of 10, for both $x$ and $y$. Each leaf matches one ordering of 2 x-steps and 2 y-steps, and there are $\binom42 = 6$ orderings.

The result is $6\times10 = \mathbf{60}$.
=== 1.55.5
Each recursive call is pushed onto the call stack. The most recent call finishes first, i.e. **last in, first out**. **Option B**.
=== 1.55.6
Naive recursive Fibonacci, $F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2)$, recomputes the same subproblems over and over, taking exponential time $\Theta(\phi^n)$. A simple loop does it in $O(n)$. That makes it the classic **bad** use of recursion. **Option B**.

=== 1.58.1
The bug is `i := k`, which should be `i := k+1`. Once $j = i+1$, we get $k = \lfloor(i+j)/2\rfloor = i$. If $a[i] < x$, then $i$ is set to $k = i$ again. Nothing changes, and since $i < j$ the loop never ends.

**It fails (loops forever)** whenever the search narrows to two adjacent positions $i, i+1$ with $a[i] < x$. In particular:

- when $x > a[N-1]$, including $x = a[N]$ (the last element) and $x$ larger than every element;
- more generally, whenever $x$ is absent and lies above the element at the left end of the final pair.

(If $x$ is smaller than everything, $j$ keeps moving left and the loop ends correctly.)
