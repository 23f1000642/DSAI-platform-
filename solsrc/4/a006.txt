=== 4.10.1
The premises are $p$ and $(p\to q)\lor(p\land(r\to q))$. Since $p$ is true:

- $p\to q$ reduces to $q$
- $p\land(r\to q)$ reduces to $r\to q$

**As a derivation:** take the two cases of the disjunction.

- **Case 1**, $p\to q$: with $p$, we get $q$, and then $r\to q$ holds for any $r$.
- **Case 2**, $p\land(r\to q)$: simplification gives $r\to q$ directly.

Either way, $r\to q$.

=== 4.10.7
Write each statement in clause form:

1. $\lnot Q(x)\lor P(x)\lor\lnot R(a)$
2. $R(a)\lor\lnot Q(a)$
3. $Q(a)$
4. $\lnot P(y)$

- 3 with 2 gives **$R(a)$**.
- 1 with $x := a$, resolved with 3, gives **$P(a)\lor\lnot R(a)$**.
- That with $R(a)$ gives **$P(a)$**.
- That with 4 ($y := a$) gives the **empty clause □**.

=== 4.10.8
Let $F = A\land(A\to(B\lor C))\land(B\to\lnot A)$. We show that F → C is a tautology, i.e. C is true in every row where F is true.

| A | B | C | A→(B∨C) | B→¬A | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | F | F |
| T | T | F | T | F | F |
| **T** | **F** | **T** | T | T | **T** |
| T | F | F | F | T | F |
| F | – | – | – | – | F (A false) |

F is true **only** in the row A = T, B = F, C = T, and **C is true there**. So C is a logical consequence of F. ∎
