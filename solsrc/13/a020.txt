=== 13.30.1
**(i)** $T_1$ has height 3 and $T_2$ has height 2, and all of $T_2$'s keys (6, 7, 8) are larger than $T_1$'s (1–5).

Merge by making $T_2$ a sibling of D's subtree, using D as the join point: replace D's right child (leaf 5) with a new internal node whose left child is 5 and whose right child is $T_2$'s root F. Then rebalance with rotations if needed.

Equivalently, create a new node joining D's subtree (height 2) and $T_2$ (height 2) at the same level. That keeps every height difference ≤ 1.

**(ii)** Walk down the taller tree's right spine to a node whose height equals the shorter tree's height (or differs by 1). Attach it there, and rebalance on the way back up with at most one rotation per level. The cost is **$O(|h_1 - h_2| + 1)$**, which is $O(\max(h_1, h_2)) = O(\log n)$.
=== 13.30.2
With $I$ internal nodes, each with 2 or 3 children, and 9 leaves:

- **All 3-children:** 9 leaves under 3 internal nodes, under 1 root. That's $I = 4$.
- **Using 2-children nodes:** 9 leaves can sit under 3 nodes (3+3+3) or 4 nodes (3+2+2+2). Then those nodes need parents: 4 nodes under 2 parents (2+2), and the 2 parents under 1 root. That's $4 + 2 + 1 = 7$.

Both $I = 4$ and $I = 7$ are possible. The level counts don't allow 5 or 6.

**A and D**.
=== 13.30.3
**Claim:** $L(n) = 2n + 1$ for a 3-ary tree with $n$ internal nodes.

- **Base case** ($n = 0$): a single node, which is one leaf. $2(0) + 1 = 1$ ✔.
- **Inductive step.** Take an internal node $v$ whose 3 children are all leaves (one exists in any finite such tree). Removing those children makes $v$ a leaf. That tree has $n - 1$ internal nodes, so by induction it has $2(n-1) + 1 = 2n - 1$ leaves. Putting the children back loses 1 leaf ($v$) and gains 3: $2n - 1 - 1 + 3 = 2n + 1$ ✔.

(The question's "2(n+1)" is a typo. The standard result is $2n + 1$, from $L = (k-1)I + 1$ with $k = 3$.)
