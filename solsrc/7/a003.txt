The non-zero eigenvalues are 2 and 3, and their product is **6**.
=== 7.5.11
The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues. If it's negative, an odd number of the eigenvalues are negative, so at least one is. **A** is true.

The others fail:

- C fails for eigenvalues −1, −1 (determinant 1).
- D fails for eigenvalues −1, −1, 5 (trace 3, determinant 5).

=== 7.5.13

=== 7.5.14

=== 7.5.15
Complex eigenvalues of a real matrix come in conjugate pairs, so the third eigenvalue is $2 - i$:
$$\det P = (2+i)(2-i)\cdot3 = 5\cdot3 = \mathbf{15}$$
=== 7.5.16
$\det(A^{-1})^T = \det A^{-1} = \frac{1}{1\cdot2\cdot4} = \frac18 = \mathbf{0.125}$.
=== 7.5.17
A is real symmetric of rank 2, so it has exactly 2 non-zero eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$. Also
$$\lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2 = 50$$

- **I:** both can't have $|\lambda| > 5$, since then their squares would add up to more than 50. Besides, the other eigenvalues are 0, which is in [−5, 5]. **True.**
- **II:** $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 5$ gives a sum of squares of exactly 50, so the largest magnitude needn't be **strictly** greater than 5. **False.**

**I only**, **option B**.
=== 7.5.18
$$P + Q = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&-2\\8&9&10\\8&8&8\end{pmatrix}$$
Row 2 − row 3 = (0, 1, 2), which is −1 × row 1. So the rows are dependent. Rows 1 and 3 are independent, so the **rank is 2**.
=== 7.5.19
2 is a root: $8 - 16 + 2a + 30 = 0$, so $a = -11$.

The polynomial is $\lambda^3 - 4\lambda^2 - 11\lambda + 30 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 15) = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 3)$.

The eigenvalues are 2, 5 and −3. The largest absolute value is **5**.
=== 7.5.20
$A = uv^T$ has rank 1. Its only non-zero eigenvalue is $v^Tu = 1 + 2 = \mathbf{3}$.
=== 7.5.21
Every eigenvector is a multiple of $(1, 4)$, so there's only **one independent eigenvector**.

- A diagonalizable $2\times2$ matrix needs two independent eigenvectors, so P **can't be diagonalized** (III ✔).
- With only one eigenvector direction, the eigenvalue must be repeated (II ✔). Distinct eigenvalues would give two independent eigenvectors.
- I isn't necessary. The repeated eigenvalue can be non-zero: take $P = \begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\-16&6\end{pmatrix}$. Its only eigenvectors are multiples of (1, 4), its eigenvalue 2 is repeated, and $\det P = 4 \ne 0$, so it **is** invertible.

**II and III**, **option D**.
